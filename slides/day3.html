What the pre-registration fixes

A group sequential plan is a contract written before day 1

The looks

How many and when, or the information fractions.

The spending

O'Brien-Fleming, Pocock or another function, with alpha.

The maximum

The longest the test may run: 28 days for T-101.

Guardrails can stop a test too. A harm guardrail is usually monitored with its own, looser boundary: stopping for harm should be easy.

Ask the room what happens if someone adds a look on day 10 that was not in the plan. With a spending function the plan can absorb an unplanned look, provided the information fraction is recomputed; without one, the error rate is no longer known.

The other family

An always-valid test lets you look and stop at any time

  • No plan of looks, no maximum duration.
  • Its statistic is a likelihood ratio averaged over possible effects; under no effect it never drifts upward on average.
  • So the chance it ever reaches 1 / alpha is at most alpha.

Names you will meet

mixture SPRT (mSPRT), confidence sequences, anytime-valid inference.

Johari, Pekelis and Walsh brought the mixture SPRT to A/B testing (course sources). GrowthBook's documentation says its sequential testing uses asymptotic confidence sequences (Waudby-Smith and colleagues), close to the generalised anytime-valid approach Spotify compared. The martingale argument is Ville's inequality; one sentence is enough for this room.

Lab 3.2, step 1

The mSPRT statistic needs the estimate, its variance and a width

v = se**2
lam = sqrt(v / (v + tau**2)) * exp(tau**2 * est**2 / (2 * v * (v + tau**2)))
stop = lam >= 1 / alpha                     # 20 for alpha = 0.05
  • tau, the mixture width, says which effects the test is tuned to: here 0.08 orders, the effect we hope for.
  • Platforms expose it as a tuning parameter; GrowthBook's default is 5,000 observations.

The formula is for a normal estimate with known variance, which holds well for differences in means at these sizes. GrowthBook parameterises the width as the sample size at which the interval is narrowest; its documentation advises setting it near the sample size at which decisions are usually made.

Interference

In a marketplace, one customer's treatment changes another's outcome

Treated orderGoes to the next free courier first
→
Same couriersBusy with treated orders
→
Control orderWaits longer than it would without the test
No interference is an assumption. A randomised comparison is unbiased only if a unit's outcome depends on its own treatment alone.

The assumption has a name, SUTVA (stable unit treatment value). It fails whenever units share a limited resource: couriers, kitchen capacity, a promotion budget, inventory, a ranking slot.

The fix

Randomise what shares the couriers: the city-hour

Cluster design

Whole cities on the new rule. Six units: hopeless power.

Switchback

Each city switches between rules hour by hour, at random: 6 cities × 14 hours × 14 days = 1,176 blocks.

Inside a block every order has the same rule, so nobody jumps a queue of the other arm.

Bojinov, Simchi-Levi and Zhao give the design and analysis of switchback experiments (course sources). Delivery platforms use switchbacks for dispatch and pricing for exactly this reason.

Lab 3.4, step 2

Estimate with city and hour fixed effects, block as the unit

fit = smf.ols("delivery_min ~ treat + C(city) + C(hour)",
              data=blocks).fit(cov_type="HC1")
fit.params["treat"], fit.bse["treat"]      # one run: -1.19, se 0.30
  • One row per city-hour block, not per order: the block was randomised.
  • Each block counts once; weight by orders when the decision is about orders.

The regression weights every city-hour equally; the global effect of Lab 3.3 weights every order. That is why the switchback's mean estimate, -1.51, sits below the per-order -1.78 in size: busy hours, where the gain is largest, count once like quiet ones.

Lab 3.4, step 4

Carryover between hours is small in this simulator, so measure it

  • Unfinished deliveries spill into the next hour, whichever rule it has.
  • Measured: switchback with carryover -1.51 minutes, without -1.49.
  • In a real city the spill can be larger: test two-hour blocks, or drop the first hour after each switch.
An honest finding. The design choice here is the weighting, not the block length. Your marketplace may differ: simulate yours.

The simulator's spill applies to both arms almost equally, which is why the bias is near zero. Do not generalise this number; generalise the method: simulate the design with and without the nuisance and compare.
