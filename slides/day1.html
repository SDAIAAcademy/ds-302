Session 1

Assignment, exposure and triggering

  • Separate assignment, exposure and the trigger condition in an event log
  • Compute a triggered effect and the diluted overall effect from it
  • Explain why counterfactual logging keeps the control group comparable
01
Assignment is not exposure

Every test log records three different events

1 · EligibleActive in the last 90 days: all 200,000 customers
→
2 · AssignedDay 0: a variant for each customer, 50/50
→
3 · ExposedAt the first search: the only moment the ranking can matter
Triggering. Analyse the customers whose exposure condition held, in both arms. The others can only add noise.

Press once per step. The trigger condition here is "searched at least once in the test period". Ask: who in this room has analysed a test over everyone assigned when only a fraction could see the change? It is the most common way a real effect disappears into noise.

The control group must be triggered too

Log the trigger in control, where nothing visible happens

Wrong

Triggered treatment customers compared with all control customers. The groups differ in who they are: searchers order more.

Right

Control logs "would have seen the AI ranking" at the same moment. Both arms are cut by the same rule, so randomisation still holds inside the cut.

The trigger must not depend on the treatment. A search happens before the ranking is shown, so the rule is safe. "Clicked a result" would not be.

In Lab 1.1 the trigger rates are 42.45% in control and 42.63% in treatment (chi-square p = 0.40): balanced, as they must be. If a feature changes who triggers (for example a trigger defined by an event the new ranking causes), the triggered comparison is biased and you must fall back to the assigned population.

Lab 1.1, step 2

Exposure is one left join and a minimum

select a.customer_id, a.variant, f.exposed_day
from assignments a
left join (select customer_id, min(day) as exposed_day
           from searches where day >= 0
           group by customer_id) f
  on f.customer_id = a.customer_id
  • Left join: a customer who never searched stays, with a NULL exposure day.
  • The same pattern counts orders per customer: coalesce(o.orders, 0) keeps the customers who did not order.

The commonest bug in this lab is an inner join to orders, which silently drops every customer with zero orders and inflates both means. Ask the room what that does to the difference: it changes the population, so it is no longer a randomised comparison. DuckDB runs the queries directly on the pandas tables; PostgreSQL would run the same SQL.

Report the triggered effect for the decision about search, and the diluted one when someone asks what it does for the whole business.

Why dilution costs power

Untriggered customers add variance and no signal

  • The effect shrinks by the trigger rate, here 0.425.
  • The noise does not shrink: 57% of the customers still vary.
  • Dividing the effect by 0.425 multiplies the customers needed by 1 / 0.425² ≈ 5.5; the assigned population is only 1 / 0.425 ≈ 2.4 times the triggered one.
5.5 vs 3.7
standard errors from zero: triggered against diluted (Lab 1.1)

The rough rule assumes similar variance in both groups; here the searchers order more and vary more, which is why the gain in z is smaller than the rule suggests. The exact comparison is the two z values on the slide.

Session 2

Sample ratio mismatch and A/A tests

  • Test a split against its plan with a chi-square test
  • Locate the cause of a mismatch by segment
  • Measure a false-positive rate with A/A reshuffles of real data
02
The first check on every test

A split that differs from its plan voids the result

  • Planned 50/50; observed 50,221 control and 49,028 treatment in T-203.
  • A chi-square test against the plan: p = 0.00015.
  • The users who went missing were not missing at random, so the remaining arms are no longer comparable.
49.4%
treatment share in T-203: small to the eye, decisive to the test
Never read the metrics of a test that fails this check. Find the cause first.

Numbers from Lab 1.2 (data/srm_tests.csv). Platforms commonly flag a split at p below 0.001; the threshold is strict because the check runs on every test, often every day. The lift of a test with a mismatch may be produced entirely by who was lost.

Where mismatches come from

Most mismatches come from what happens after assignment

Lost

The treatment crashes or loads slower on one build, so its users never log an exposure.

Filtered

A bot filter or a data-quality rule removes more users from one arm.

Redirected

A treatment that redirects or reloads assigns some users twice or not at all.

Diagnose by splitting the counts by every dimension that could lose users unevenly: platform, build, day, browser.

In Lab 1.2 the split by app build shows the cause at once: build 7.4 has a treatment share of 46.4% (p far below 0.001) while every other build is near 50%. The split by day shows a treatment share below 50% on 11 of 14 days, which points to a permanent cause, not an outage.

A/A tests

Reshuffle real data to measure the false-win rate you really have

  1. Take 28 days of pre-period revenue per customer.
  2. Split the customers at random into two halves.
  3. Run the analysis you plan to use. Record p.
  4. Repeat 500 times. Count p below 0.05.
5.8%
false wins over 500 reshuffles (expected 5% ± 1.9)

The customer-level Welch test is calibrated on Sufrah's heavy-tailed revenue.

From Lab 1.2 step 4, full mode. An A/A rate well above 5% means the analysis is wrong for the data, most often because the unit of analysis is smaller than the unit of randomisation. The stretch shows it: split by customer, test at the order level, and the rate rises. Day 2 session 3 repairs it with the delta method.

A trust problem in time

A new feature can win because it is new

  • Novelty: customers try the change because it is different; the lift fades.
  • Primacy: customers used to the old version resist; the lift grows.
  • Check the effect by week since first exposure, not only in total.

Day 4

Luqma, the chat assistant, lifts orders by 5.8% in week one and by nothing measurable in week four. Lab 4.1 measures it.

Reweight the treatment arm up to 50% and report the adjusted lift

Why. A mismatch means assignment did not work as planned, so the arms are no longer comparable. No analysis of the outcome repairs that.

Metrics for AI features

  • Choose a primary metric that the change can move and that matters to the decision
  • Set latency, cost and harm guardrails with thresholds from baseline data

03
Metric roles

One primary metric decides; guardrails can only veto

RoleQuestion it answersT-101 example
PrimaryDid the change do what it is for?Orders per triggered customer, 28 days
GuardrailDid it break something we will not trade?Search latency, model cost, complaints
DiagnosticWhy did it move?Clicks on the first result, searches per session
A guardrail is not a second primary. A guardrail that "improved" does not rescue a primary that did not move.

Ask the room for the primary metric of T-101 before showing the table. Revenue per customer is tempting; Lab 1.4 shows it needs about twice the traffic of orders per customer for the same 2% lift. Diagnostics explain, they never decide.

What an AI feature adds

A model call adds latency, cost and new harms

Latency

Every search waits for the model. Measure the tail: p95, not the mean.

Cost

A price per call, paid on every search, forever. Measure per 1,000 searches.

Harm

Wrong or unavailable items ranked first, unsafe answers, unfair exposure. Measure complaints and flags.

The SDAIA Generative AI Guidelines ask that the risks of a generative feature be assessed and monitored; a harm guardrail is how a test does it.

The keyword ranking costs almost nothing per search; the AI ranking costs about SAR 5.8 per 1,000 searches in the ramp. Over a year of searches that is a number finance will ask about. Sources: SDAIA Generative AI Guidelines for Government and for the Public (course sources).

Writing a guardrail

A guardrail is a metric, a direction and a number, written before the test

{"metric": "search latency p95 (ms), treatment minus control",
 "kind": "latency", "direction": "at most", "threshold": 300},
{"metric": "inference cost per 1,000 searches (SAR)",
 "kind": "cost", "direction": "at most", "threshold": 8.0},
{"metric": "complaints per 1,000 orders, upper 95% bound of the difference",
 "kind": "harm", "direction": "at most", "threshold": 1.0, "harm": true}
Harm guardrails use the interval. The upper bound must stay under the line: "no evidence of harm" is not enough.

The harm guardrail is a non-inferiority test: the change passes only if the interval rules out a rise of more than one complaint per 1,000 orders. Ask why the latency threshold is on the difference and the cost threshold on the treatment level: latency is relative to what customers have now, cost is a budget.

Session 4

Power for heavy-tailed metrics

  • Compute a sample size with the two-sample formula
  • Estimate power by simulation from historical data
  • Explain when the formula and the simulation disagree for a heavy-tailed metric
04
From the pre-work

Sample size grows with the variance and with one over the effect squared

n_per_arm = 2 * (z_0.975 + z_0.80)**2 * sd**2 / delta**2
          = 2 * 7.85 * sd**2 / delta**2
  • Halve the effect: four times the customers.
  • Halve the standard deviation: a quarter of them.
  • Day 2 is about the second line.

This recaps DS-205 and the pre-work. If the entry quiz showed gaps, spend two minutes on the meaning of power: the chance that a test with the planned size detects an effect of the planned size.

Power by simulation

Simulate the test you will run on the data you have

for _ in range(sims):
    c = rng.choice(values, n)                  # control arm from history
    t = rng.choice(values, n) * (1 + lift)     # treatment arm, lifted
    hits += ttest_ind(t, c, equal_var=False).pvalue < 0.05
power = hits / sims
  • Keeps the real distribution, tail included.
  • Works for any analysis: capping, triggering, a sequential plan, a ratio metric.

The multiplicative lift assumes every customer changes by the same proportion. It is a modelling choice; an additive lift or a lift concentrated in light users gives other answers, which is a good stretch question.

What the formula hides. Its standard deviation came from one sample. A sample that missed a few giant orders gives a sigma, and a size, that are too small.
