Capping, also called winsorising

Count each order up to a cap set from both arms pooled

cap = orders.value.quantile(0.99)   # pooled
v = orders.value.clip(upper=cap)
revenue = v.groupby(orders.customer_id).sum()

Never set the cap per arm. A cap that depends on the treatment can create a difference on its own.

Cap percentiles are from the pooled test-period orders of triggered customers. In a real programme the cap is fixed in the pre-registration, from historical data, before the test starts. Ask: what does a cap at the 99th percentile mean for a customer who places one SAR 2,000 catering order? It counts as SAR 337.

The price of a cap

A capped metric estimates a different, smaller effect

  • The ranking adds orders, some of them group orders.
  • Capped, those orders count for less: the effect on the capped metric is smaller than on revenue.
  • That gap is a bias if the decision is about revenue.

Only in synthetic data

The generator knows every customer's orders under both arms, so the true effect of each cap is exact. No real test can measure this bias; a simulation can.

This is why the course uses synthetic data: the potential outcomes (_potential in the stream) let you compute the true uncapped effect, 17.91 SAR per triggered customer, and the true effect under each cap. In practice you estimate the bias the same way, by simulating on historical data.

Writing it down

Pre-register the cap, report the uncapped result beside it

  1. The percentile and the data it comes from: pooled, before the test.
  2. What the metric now means: revenue with each order counted up to SAR 337.
  3. The uncapped estimate in the appendix, so nobody wonders what was hidden.
Capping is not deleting. The large orders stay in the data; they just stop deciding the interval.

Deleting outliers changes the population; capping keeps every customer and limits each order's influence. Ask the room whether their own dashboards cap, and at what. Many platforms offer a percentile cap as a metric setting; check the documentation of the one you use.

Session 2

Regression adjustment

  • Estimate the CUPED coefficient from pre-period data
  • Compute adjusted and unadjusted intervals
  • Report the variance reduction as an effective sample-size gain
02
The idea

Last month's orders predict this month's, whatever the arm

0.85
correlation between orders in the 28 pre-period days and the 28 test days, triggered customers
  • Most of the variance between customers is habit, and habit was there before the test.
  • Remove what the pre-period predicts and the remaining noise is small.
  • The pre-period cannot carry the effect: it ended before assignment.

Correlation from Lab 2.2, full mode. For revenue the correlation is lower, 0.59, because group orders are not habits. Ask what a correlation of zero would mean: nothing to gain.

CUPED

Subtract theta times the centred pre-period metric

theta = cov(y, x) / var(x)       # both arms pooled
y_cuped = y - theta * (x - x.mean())
diff_ci(y_cuped, t)
  • Same expected difference: the term has mean zero in each arm.
  • Variance multiplied by 1 - rho²: here 0.27.
  • theta = 0.864 for orders in T-101.

The method was published by Deng, Xu, Kohavi and Walker (2013) under the name CUPED. GrowthBook's documentation describes the same estimator, with theta from a regression of post-exposure on pre-exposure data pooled across the variations, and 14 days of pre-exposure data by default. Sources: the CUPED paper and GrowthBook's CUPED page (course sources).

This is LO1's evidence: unadjusted and adjusted intervals with the effective sample-size gain. The estimate moved from 0.253 to 0.226 because the treatment arm happened to have slightly heavier orderers before the test (6.449 against 6.418 orders); the adjustment corrects that chance imbalance. Both are estimates of the same effect.

The same thing, as a regression

Regression adjustment gives the same number to four decimals

d = df.assign(xc=df.orders_pre - df.orders_pre.mean())
fit = smf.ols("orders ~ treat + xc", data=d).fit(cov_type="HC2")
fit.params["treat"], fit.bse["treat"]    # 0.2261, 0.0241
  • The regression form extends to several covariates: pre-period orders, revenue, city.
  • Use robust standard errors; centre the covariate so the treatment coefficient is the average effect.

With one covariate and theta pooled, CUPED and the regression with a common slope are the same estimator. With many covariates, keep them few and measured before assignment; a covariate measured after assignment can carry part of the effect.

Reporting the saving

Report the gain as customers: it is what the business buys

Orders, 28 pre-period days
3.7× the customers
Revenue, 28 pre-period days
about 1.5×

Gain = 1 / (1 - rho²). Orders: rho = 0.85. Revenue: rho = 0.59, so 1 / (1 - 0.34) ≈ 1.5.

For T-101: a revenue test that needed 55 days on Day 1 needs far fewer with capping and adjustment together.

The revenue gain is computed from the correlation printed in the lab's look-at step (0.586). Capping first raises it, because the uncorrelated group orders lose weight. The stretch compares 14 and 28 pre-period days.

Session 3

Ratio metrics

  • Explain why a session-level interval is too narrow when customers are randomised
  • Compute a delta-method interval for a ratio metric
  • Check it with a bootstrap over customers
03
Ratio metrics

Orders per session: the numerator and the denominator both vary

  • Randomised: customers. Counted: sessions and orders.
  • The metric: all orders over all sessions in the arm, a ratio of two sums.
  • Not the mean of each customer's own rate, which weights a one-session customer like a fifty-session one.
0.2036 vs 0.1964
orders per session, treatment and control: +3.7%

From Lab 2.3: 85,080 triggered customers and 2,807,935 sessions in the test period. Clicks per page view, revenue per order and orders per session are all ratio metrics. The trap is always the same: the unit of analysis is smaller than the unit of randomisation.

The trap

Treating sessions as independent makes the interval 2.6 times too narrow

Naive, sessions as trials
± 0.0009
Delta method, by customer
± 0.0024
Why it matters. An interval 2.6 times too narrow turns noise into wins: the A/A test of Day 1 would show far more than 5% false wins.

Standard errors from Lab 2.3: naive 0.00048, delta method 0.00124. Sessions of one customer are alike: same habits, same appetite. The binomial formula assumes 2.8 million independent trials when there are 85,080 independent customers.

The delta method

Compute the variance of a ratio from customer-level sums

o, s = arm.orders, arm.sessions          # one row per customer
r = o.mean() / s.mean()
c = np.cov(o, s)
var_r = (c[0,0] - 2*r*c[0,1] + r**2*c[1,1]) / (n * s.mean()**2)
  • A first-order Taylor expansion of mean(O) / mean(S) around its expectation.
  • Every term is per customer, the unit that was randomised.

Deng, Knoblich and Lu (2018) describe the method for metric analytics and its pitfalls (course sources). The formula is per arm; the variance of the difference is the sum of the two. For a relative difference apply the method once more (the stretch).

The check

A bootstrap over customers agrees within 4%

MethodStandard error95% interval of the difference

This is LO2's evidence: two agreeing intervals and a note on why the naive one is too narrow. The bootstrap resamples the unit that was randomised; resampling sessions would reproduce the naive interval's mistake.

Session 4

Stratification

  • Distinguish stratified assignment from post-stratification
  • Compute a post-stratified estimate and its standard error
  • Judge when strata help and when pre-period data helps more
04
Stratification

Use the strata in the design, or in the analysis

Stratified assignment

Randomise 50/50 inside each city. The arms cannot differ in their city mix.

Post-stratification

Randomise simply, then estimate city by city and weight by each city's share.

Both remove the noise that comes from differences between cities. Neither can remove more than the cities explain.

For large tests the two are nearly equivalent. Stratified assignment matters more for small tests, or when a platform must guarantee balance on a key dimension.

Lab 2.4, step 3

Estimate per city, weight by each city's share

g = d.groupby(["city", "treat"]).orders.agg(["mean", "var", "size"]).unstack("treat")
w = (g["size"][0] + g["size"][1]) / len(d)
est = (w * (g["mean"][1] - g["mean"][0])).sum()
se = sqrt((w**2 * (g["var"][1]/g["size"][1] + g["var"][0]/g["size"][0])).sum())

Walk through the four lines. The weights are each city's share of all triggered customers, so the estimate averages over the same population as the simple difference.

Checkpoint

When does stratifying by city pay off?

Sufrah plans a 2,000-customer pilot in two cities whose customers order at very different rates. Which design reduces the variance of the estimate most?

A simple 50/50 randomisation, analysed as a simple difference
Strata for every district, analysed per district
Randomisation within each city, analysed with the city strata
A separate test in each city, reported side by side
Why. When the strata explain much of the variance and the test is small, randomising within strata prevents imbalance and removes the between-strata noise.

Answer: C. Contrast with T-101: 85,000 customers in six cities that explain 1.2% of the variance, where strata change nothing.
